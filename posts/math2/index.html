I thought about both of them when I said to Saint-Laurent I wanted to make a film, just as Plisnier returned to his old love, revolutionary politics.

I wondered: “Could I make a film knowing how to make it now like I did, but also as if I was now transported to make a film like Melville had done when he made Le Silence de la mer?”

— Jean-Luc Godard

He wanted to get back to the old ways of making films, the ways he started out with, but with the distance of experience.

— Fabrice Aragno, on Godard

One of the reasons it is difficult to write about Godard is because it is difficult to know what Godard you are referring to. Is it the flashy Godard of the early years, the storyteller, the Godard so full of joie de vivre? Is it the political Godard of the ‘70s, estranged from his community and the people of his homeland? The more conventional return to form, the experimental sentimentality of Historie(s) or Eloge de l’amour? The late-period essayist? The number of iterations Godard cycled through were so multitudinous, so vibrant, that pinning him down as anything but a master of the visual form is close to impossible; his union with the moving image was so foundational to his being that it is hardly possible to imagine anyone who fundamentally knew more about what cinema was, is and will continue to be. This was a man whose very thought proceeded in images.

I watched a Godard at Berkeley the other day at a special BAMPFA screening. To my right were two old French women I had a little chat with in my own broken recitation of their language; they were thoroughly amused, but encouraging nevertheless. The film was Bande à part. I was hungry and frustrated—a tram derail and a slow cheeseburger order had left me in a poor mood, overall, but all that business vanished from my mind the moment that Anna Karina’s face entered the image. It is hard to watch a film, especially one as joyous as this, and not find yourself far from the roiling crowds of despair and the prophets of your ruin but rather in, in true Sherlock Jr. fashion—a film that I now know was deeply loved by Godard, so bare are its influences in Bande à part—into the throes of the past. And yet even as you are there, transfixed, you can find a deep and fundamental joy in their hearts that is so unwilling to find itself a place within your own.

They made Casablanca in 1942. There was a war—the very worst—and they, in turn, responded with Casablanca. How could that be? How could those people—the very same species, people not very different from us, not far removed either in space or time—how could those people see the unending death and devastation around them and respond with a movie so relentless in its thirst for honor and devotion, so uniquely committed to love and duty? How could they find it in themselves to care so, so much?

The truth is that no matter what Godard you care to discuss that common strand uniting all his iterations never failed—the strand that held nothing but care and utmost respect for cinema and its masters, and knew, deep down, that it was worth trying to make something new out of film, not because it was easy, but because it was hard.

not because it is easy, but because it is hard

I suppose at this point mathematicians are in something not far from shambles. ‘Gradually, and then suddenly’—so says Mike Campbell about the arrival of his bankruptcy in Hemingway’s The Sun Also Rises, and the quote is perhaps one of those rare generalities that can apply to any number of situations. It is also one that is apt for this particular course of affairs—the plague of LLMs in mathematics, and the deluge of automation that is following. Until this June I had never even seen a mathematician using LLMs to make novel discoveries in his field. Then suddenly it is October and I can see all my favorite open problems falling, uniformly, like dominoes. There is some sense in which this was inevitable, and about a month ago I placed a bet with a good friend that such a thing would occur on timescales not very far from those we have already hit today1. That particular bet has not been collected—not yet, anyway—but my moral preparation for living in a world inhabited not by mathematicians, but by buttons which when pressed reveal the infinitude of mathematical truth has more-or-less reached completion. The method in which this manifests is a profound numbness. In fact I have never been more keenly aware that in a rather meaningful sense these solutions are, ultimately, just a bunch of words on a page. That they carry profound insight in them is a function not of their inherent worth, but of the hairless apes that devised them, and those who cared enough to factory-line them.

How odd to think so! It seems that I must welcome such a renaissance of mathematical knowledge, and yet all I feel is apathy. In fact I very much do not seem to care about the whole business at all, beyond the occasional flicker at the manner of result. One can say that I am disappointed in God—not because of this whole business that only other humans, not He, has wrought, and undoubtedly forms part of the grand, cosmic plan He has for all of us—for allowing there to exist a $n(\log n)^{1-2^{-182}}$ algorithm for integer multiplication2, but that truly is, these days, secondary. By and large the whole business feels very much inconsequential. So be it, then, let them put up whatever number of papers they want. When the ‘all at once’ does arrive—in consequential terms, as in, if I get laid off, or if some sort of catastrophe occurs—it will arrive, and there is little that can be done about it.

I suspect in many ways this sounds to the uninformed like a form of denial. Admittedly it would be good to have a little bit of denial—but then again, the cat is out of the bag, and it is quite a perilous cat that people have unleashed, even if it never really gains any further capabilities beyond solving mathematical problems at all, and attempting to deny this fact does not make it go away. But what can come off as denial is only a natural response to a profound question of meaning. I have been, at various times over the last few months, chastised for being rather gloomy in the face of what appears to be ‘progress.’ Surely more is better, right? Do the great mathematical minds of the world not want this? Is this not what they desired—that their problems be solved? Besides the usual arguments about adapting3 and progress for progress’ sake, the one I am faced with the most is this bizarre fixation on democratization and skillset transfer: that in truth this lowers the barrier of entry into mathematics, and now anyone can make a novel contribution. Isn’t that great?

And indeed it is tempting to think so. But one of the problems of interacting with people who are limited in their understanding of mathematics is that they know little of what they are indeed missing out on. And while I may sound accusatory or dismissive, the reason they know little of what they are missing out on is precisely because they do not know mathematics.

knowledge and wisdom

I dislike quoting from the internet, but these unprecedented times call for unprecedented measures. Here is a sentiment I saw expressed on Substack the other day:

When I tell you that AI has utterly ruined my job, it is an understatement. Teaching writing to students who see no value in the skill, and consequently no value in what I offer them is just torturous. What used to be a collegial, fun engagement is now just an exercise in adversarial one-upping. Exhausting to lose every skill and sensibility one has worked for over three decades.

It is hard not to pity the woman who wrote this: to see some of the things you care about so deeply discarded by the wayside due to simple economics, and to see the generational tide turn in a rather ugly way. The kids do not know how to write, nor do they care how to write. They are college students—surely they must be interested in their own mental development, in their intellectual flourishing? How could they do this if they do not know how to write?

The answer, of course, seems to be the same as that of why they don’t do anything these days: writing is not an activity which can lead them to a materially successful life, and therefore there is little reason to pay to do it. This is a hard pill to swallow, but it is also not unprecedented. Certainly people do not go to school for artisanship en masse, not now that there are factories to create goods—there are factories to create writing, now, too, and so one does not need to be able to do it to be employed. But wondering if one needs to learn how to write in these days is not particularly different than wondering if one needs to learn how to do mathematics: it seems like the computer can do it, really, and therefore it’s unclear how valuable it is to learn. And there has always been truth to this. For the vast majority of human history, the masses were illiterate. Reading and writing was not the profoundly valuable skill it is today; indeed it was a skill cultivated by monks and intellectuals, and even when writing began arriving to the masses post the industrial revolution the actual act of literature being something that anyone—not just the English nobility, for instance—could partake in took even longer. Today you can go to college and get a degree in writing, just as you can go to college and get a degree in mathematics. In that sense what we appear to be going towards is a post-literate society.

Of course, one can hardly argue that not knowing writing and mathematics does not lead to mental decline.

Because isn’t that the goal? It seems on some fundamental level important that we as a society progress towards those ideals of antiquity that are so beloved to us: a vast and educated citizenry presided over by philosopher-kings, where each man partakes in his own manner of intellectual and physical flourishing. A society comprised of healthy and resilient men and women that consists of partaking in art and pleasure, where each person knows their duty to society and carries it out obediently not out of force but out of their own wise choice. Such a society is utopian and may ultimately even be contrary to human nature, but it is a worthwhile goal nevertheless. And yet it so occurs that the children do not want to learn how to write. Their objection is the same as that of mathematics: why? Why do it when a computer can do it? How does addition help me if we live in a world with a calculator?

It is only natural to ask these questions. Teachers, of course, respond with various pragmatisms, but telling a child who does not know writing and mathematics these answers is like trying to stuff the whole world into a telescope. In truth the answer is the same as it has ever been, to treat children like children and do unto them what they did unto all those who had similar objections a hundred years ago: make them eat their veggies. Perhaps attempting to convince them that these things are important is not just a failing activity, but it is something actively detrimental to their well-being: are things only good if their obvious benefits can be laid bare in material fashion right down on the blackboard? There is no choice in the matter—perhaps ‘you must do it because I said you must’ is good enough of an answer? To explain to someone who does not know how to write the obvious benefits of knowing how to write—the way it organizes your thoughts, the way it transforms your rhetoric, the ability it gives you to parse increasingly complex inner frameworks, the way it allows for far superior communication ability—is indeed difficult, because the skills required to understand what knowing how to write does for a person are precisely those which are present only in those who do, indeed, know how to write.

It is most certainly a curious state of affairs. But then so are all such matters concerned with the transference of wisdom.

One of the reasons I talk about writing so much while thinking of mathematics is because those things, to me, do not seem unrelated. There are many who will point out that there is a certain skill in the presentation and writing of mathematical results on its own—a phrase widely touted and even a major objective of study among the mathematicians of old, who really were very concerned with what ‘proof’ and ‘truth’ really entailed, but generally discarded in practice by the working mathematician, who favors complex problem-solving over presentation. There are many strange reasons why this is the case. The classical one, of course, has to do with the fact that academia presents an optimization problem—it carries within itself its own currency, one of prestige, and the way to succeed in that game is to gain the recognition of one’s peers. More pragmatically, this currency forms the basis of hiring decisions, and one’s peers of course heavily value those who contribute to the opus of human knowledge through solving harder and harder problems and making increasing amounts of breakthroughs over presenting a few problems well. This is only natural. But there is a certain art to presenting mathematics, and it is one that is baked into its culture by the nature of its beings. Why do mathematicians have their own lingo? The colloquial usage of terms like ‘trivial’ and ‘epsilon probability’ follows from their extraordinary ability to describe phenomena—even beyond mathematical—with great precision. This phenomenon of understanding the preciseness of your rhetoric is one that has great overlap between mathematics and writing. To write is to distill the essence of one’s disorganized and, frankly, irrational means of thought into a continuous series of words that betray the essence of the idea to anyone who chooses to gaze upon it. So is mathematical proof. The way that intellectual thought organizes itself into rhetorical arguments is the same way that mathematical feeling and intuition organizes itself into the concreteness of lemma-arguments and theorem statements. And, much like writing, the only way people can get better at mathematics is by doing mathematics. The wisdom gained therein from deep mathematical thought is a curious sort of wisdom that is quite divorced from that of mere knowledge.

If one reads widely and deliberately one inordinately begins to draw directions between seemingly unrelated things and starts seeing the essential patterns among them, and one who does mathematics, can, too, start to understand various phenomena with much intensity. To give my own embarrassing example, my group theory class in undergrad was well-motivated, and for a long time the professor made us write down long tables of groups such as $Q_8$ or $A_4$, along with painstakingly listing the actions from the group to itself. It seemed like a profoundly boring exercise, not dissimilar to learning your times-tables. But understanding the structure of these groups indeed has an effect not very different from that of your times-tables: the ability to multiply numbers at will indeed has quite a strong effect on anyone doing higher mathematics, at least as an endless source of examples and tests and deep understanding of those numbers. Similarly, knowing $Q_8$ like an old friend provides you with the ability to reason about groups at deep levels and learn much more context about the levels you are operating on. Here is a ‘thought’ I had about a year or two after that class:

The Sylow theorems are a partial converse to Lagrange’s theorem.

Many would find this statement obvious, or entirely trivial. But I arrived at this conclusion precisely while attempting to answer a question about groups; indeed I had essentially forgotten what the Sylow theorems were, and it was a last-ditch attempt to throw large-scale machinery at the problem in the hopes of arriving at something from it. So I looked them up, and the above thought struck me. I knew that there was in fact no complete converse to Lagrange’s theorem because I had an intuitive knowledge of my pesky friend $A_4$, and so when I understood that the Sylow theorems formed a partially successful attempt to construct that converse my understanding of them suddenly become far deeper—in the past I had not even remembered the statement, but this flash of understanding allowed me to derive the proof of the first theorem entirely on my own. There are many components to this knowledge; that converses are desirable on their own, for instance, which someone has to have encountered via attempts to solve a problem to realize the generality of, or that even a partial converse can prove useful, or why only a partial converse succeeds in this setting—not merely because of the existence of a counterexample in $A_4$ but the fundamental structure of $A_4$ that prevents the existence of one (and indeed, exploiting such structures is how one arrives at the proof).

This kind of deep, structural knowledge of mathematics is something that is easy to approximate but difficult to communicate. It is an exercise in mathematical wisdom that allows one to grasp the essential quality of the structures one is dealing with. The problem is that a single read-through of a textbook does not provide one with this knowledge, even if one is able to solve the occasional problem using it (and indeed solving the occasional problem is exactly how universities and schools attempt to teach mathematics—it is not the most optimal solution, perhaps, but it is about the best that can be done). It arrives when it arrives.

the devil of gratification

Commercial mathematics does not reward deep work. Commercial writing does, sometimes—it is similarly a skill to develop writing, and often many insightful ideas are unlocked within the process of writing itself, knowing that what you are writing is in some sense ‘good’ and that feeling of clicking together—but the vast majority of writing is for minor reasons. Not bad reasons, per se, but minor reasons—writing a text, for instance, or writing an email. Most people these days do not write more than that.

But the act of understanding mathematics is such a personal and intimate one that it does not seem to have much in the way of material reward at all. Nevertheless, it is both very difficult and very easy indeed to communicate why that activity is worth doing, and is probably summarized by my favorite Seinfeld quote of all time:

George: Why would I spend seven dollars to see a movie that I could watch on TV?

Kramer: Well, why go to a fine restaurant, when you can just stick something in the microwave? Why go to the park and fly a kite, when you can just pop a pill?

In truth, the rewards of activities are only the essential fundamentals about them that can be garnered through an amount of wisdom that is inculcated by those who do the activity as a being unto itself. There are many who run long-distances, but the true long-distance runner is not the one who runs the longest, but the one who runs solely for for its own sake4, and not to present to the world that one is a runner. There are scruffily-dressed Frenchmen that run miles in four minutes and stop for smoke breaks after each fifth that can outrun steady trainers, not because they have it in them this optimal desire for improvement, but because to them the activity is merely the activity. That is not to say they don’t have a desire for improvement—it is simply secondary to doing the activity. Magnus Carlsen worked hard, but he did not work hard because he wanted to show to everyone that he was the best at chess, nor did he work optimally, nor did he play the perfect games. He simply loved Chess too much to not. So is true about Michael Jordan, and so is true about Paul McCartney, and so is true about Alexander Grothendeick. And so is true about the old men who sit under the trees of the Jardin du Luxembourg and play chess—not because they want to be the best but because they love the game, and because they possess the insightful wisdom of those who have dedicated themselves to it. Indeed, listen to then watch games between genius prodigies. Their appreciation and understanding operates on a level far beyond that of the competitor.

What I say is banal, but what I say is also fundamentally human. What are we doing if we are not trying to achieve human transcendence? The ability to be lost in the playing of guitar, to experience runner’s high, to be locked into a sweaty game with a rival, to find oneself mesmerized by the beauty of the Nullstellansatz—is that not what being human is all about?

And then one wonders why those who feel this intimate sublimity of the human experience resist the loss of their communities in the face of ‘progress.’

Of course, there is no one more dangerous in that regard than one who devalues this foundational experience of humanity in favor of playing God. It is fun to play God, but that fun quickly gets boring, just as it is fun to eat nothing but pizza at first until on the fifth day you realize you have turned quite sickly indeed, and the thought of putting another slice in your mouth fills you with horror, and you realize that even the old head of broccoli that you so despised now looks profoundly appealing in the face of this fatty sludge. To say that you are doing mathematics by pressing buttons certainly can provide that little piece of pleasure that pizza does, but without the deep understanding of mathematical wisdom it becomes an experience scarcely different from pressing a button on a jack-in-the-box and watching the clown pop out—fun at first, but ultimately meaningless. There is only meaning to endeavors insofar as humans do them, just as there is only meaning to art insofar as humans make it.

In short, the difficult question of whether mathematics survives this assault is one that, to me, has a simple answer: times of abundance are times of decadence, and the act of doing mathematics is only meaningful in the midst of being unable to do it. We are a decadent generation, so prone to enriching our material selves that we forget what it is to do things slow and with patience and with profound and transformative love for the thing in and of itself, the ding an sich, so prone to acquiring the cheap thrills of ‘understanding’ through watching videos and ignoring the touch of wise teachers and great thinkers, so conceited, in fact, that we forget that despite all the essentials of technology the only way to ever truly learn mathematics is the way it has always been—alone on a table with a pencil and a paper in hand, perhaps a book, deciphering the enigmatic texts within and gaining the deep knowledge of generations past. One may dislike doing so. One may even despise it—but that is okay, for it is not easy to gain this wisdom, and it really is only by sailing across the long ocean that one can arrive at the promised land. There truly are no shortcuts, and if one has been taken, you can rest assured that it is not Eden that you have arrived at but afar from your path onto Calypso’s isle where eating the lotuses will never ease your longing for the shore.

And you can go on again and again and again refining and even rebelling against the wisdom of the ancients, but time and again their words prove prescient beyond all recognition:

It is not the destination that is the point, but the journey.

All so many words for a lesson so short, yet a lesson that we as humans are so painstakingly wont to discard that we may yet forget that Godard quote all the way at the beginning, the words of a dying man as he remembered the follies of his youth and determined that what he loved, more than anything, was the film, the motion picture as-is, and after all his years of understanding and developing the taste for cinema that so defined his own career he realized that there was nothing he wanted more than to make a simple, a basic film, one not so different from Le Silence de la Mer—a film for people and about people, a film about love and hope, a film that preceded the entirety of his own ambitions. ‘The old ways of making films’—perhaps the only ones.
